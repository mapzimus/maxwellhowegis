Day 1 · Points

Massachusetts Lighthouses, by Age

Every standing lighthouse tower on the Massachusetts coast, Cape Ann to Nantucket, colored by the year it was built. Four in five went up after the Lighthouse Board formed in 1852. The glow around each light is how far it reaches.

Data: OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL), USCG Light List, Wikipedia, NOAA NCEI Coastal Relief Model
Made with: QGIS, Illustrator

Day 2 · Lines

New England’s Rivers and Where They Drain

Every stream of Strahler order 3 and up, colored by the river system it belongs to. The biggest networks get a color each, and everything else that reaches the Atlantic, the Hudson included, shares one pale blue. Line width follows stream order. The Saint John, Champlain and St. Francis networks cross into Canada before they reach the sea.

Data: NHDPlus V2.1 (U.S. EPA and USGS), Terrain Tiles, Natural Earth, US Census Bureau, OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL)
Made with: RustGIS, Python, Illustrator

Day 3 · Polygons

New England’s Towns and the Year Each Began

1,522 towns and cities colored by the year each was first chartered or incorporated. Blue towns came before the Revolution and red ones after. Gold is 1760 to 1789, when most of Vermont was chartered. The 81 grey areas are townships and gores that never incorporated. A commenter caught Providence showing its 1832 city charter, so I checked every date against a second source and fixed the ones using the wrong definition.

Data: US Census TIGER/Line 2025, Wikipedia municipality lists (CC BY-SA), Vermont charter records, OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL)
Made with: Python, Illustrator

Day 4 · Clusters

Charted Wrecks of Cape Cod, the Islands and the South Coast

216 wrecks on today’s NOAA navigational charts, from Mount Hope Bay to the outer Cape. DBSCAN (5 km radius, 5 wreck minimum) puts 115 of them into 11 clusters. These are charted wrecks only. The number of ships actually lost here runs into the thousands, and most are broken up, buried, or not on a chart.

Data: NOAA Office of Coast Survey ENC, NOAA NCEI Coastal Relief Model, US Census Bureau
Made with: R (sf, dbscan, ggplot2), Illustrator

Day 5 · Senses: Sight

Peak Foliage in Northern New England

A cloud-free fall mosaic from 47 Sentinel-2 scenes across 8 satellite passes. Each pixel is the median of the three clear views closest to that spot’s expected peak, which comes about five days earlier for every degree north. The inset shows which pass each patch came from.

Data: Contains modified Copernicus Sentinel data 2026
Made with: Python (rasterio, numpy, scipy), Illustrator

Day 6 · Vintage

Railroad Map of New England, 1911 and Today

New England had 7,602 miles of railroad at its 1911 peak. 3,904 miles, 51 percent, still carry trains. New Hampshire lost the most (65 percent) and Connecticut the least (40 percent). A 1911 segment counts as in service if a running OpenStreetMap rail line lies within 150 meters. Drawn as a page from a 1911 railroad atlas.

Data: Jeremy Atack, US Railroads 1826–1911 GIS; OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL)
Made with: Python (geopandas, matplotlib)

Day 7 · 10 minute map

Pizza in Connecticut

The ratio of pizza shops to people in every Connecticut town, kept plain because the theme is a timed map. 1,287 pizza places in 169 towns come to about one for every 2,900 people. Union has the most per person, with one shop for 805 residents. New Haven has the most shops, 55. 20 towns have none mapped, which means none in the data, not no pizza.

Data: OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL), Overture Maps Foundation (CDLA-Permissive-2.0), US Census Bureau PEP Vintage 2024
Made with: QGIS

Day 8 · Utopia

Boston’s Rapid Transit

My fantasy Boston subway map, drawn like the posters on station walls. A four-track tunnel I call The BIGGER Dig joins North Station and South Station at a new Boston Central. The map has 8 rapid transit lines, more than 220 stops, 11 commuter rail lines and 14 ferry routes. NERTA is an agency I made up. This is a fan map and has nothing to do with the MBTA.

Data: MBTA V3 API, OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL), US Census TIGER/Line
Made with: Python (matplotlib, geopandas)

Day 9 · Urban-rural

Distance to the Nearest Traffic Light

About 13,000 traffic signal locations in New England, and the straight-line distance from every point of land to the nearest one. Blinking lights are not counted. Half the land is within about 7 miles of a light. The farthest spot, in Piscataquis County, Maine, is 59.6 miles from one. 90 percent of people live on 26 percent of the land, in towns averaging under 3 miles from a light.

Data: OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL), MaineDOT, NHDOT, VTrans, MassDOT, CTDOT, NYSDOT, city signal lists for Boston, Cambridge, Nashua, Burlington and Norwalk, US Census Bureau
Made with: Python (geopandas, scipy, matplotlib)

Day 11 · Senses: Sound

New England Places Everybody Pronounces Wrong

179 towns, rivers, lakes and mountains spelled the way locals say them: WUUS-ter, GLOS-ter, LEM-in-ster. Capitals mark the stressed syllable and the worst offenders are numbered. Every pronunciation has two sources.

Data: Wikipedia IPA, local news and town sources, US Census Bureau, OpenStreetMap
Made with: Python (geopandas, matplotlib)

Day 12 · Power

How Electricity Moves Through New England

Massachusetts used more than twice the electricity it generated in 2025. This shows where the rest comes from. Seven border crossings carried 16.1 TWh in and 4.8 TWh out between October 2025 and September 2026, and the new NECEC line from Québec brought a net 5.9 TWh. Inside New England, power runs north to south and toward Boston. 1,440 power plants are drawn, each fuel with its own color and shape.

Data: ISO New England, EIA, NYISO, Canada Energy Regulator, HIFLD, OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL), Natural Earth
Made with: Python (geopandas), three.js

Day 13 · Interactions

Traffic Circles, Rotaries, and Roundabouts of New England

A specimen sheet of 72 round maps. The 55 circles at least 300 feet across are ranked from roundest to least round at the same scale. The rest are famous small ones, one-way loops around town greens, and five that were removed or rebuilt, drawn as they were. The arc on each rim is the roundness score, so a closed rim is a perfect circle.

Data: OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL), Kittelson & Associates roundabout database, USGS, MassGIS and RIGIS aerial photos
Made with: Python (geopandas, shapely, matplotlib)

Day 14 · Borgesian map

The Buildings of New England

5,349,211 building footprints. Each 285-foot square is shaded by the share of it covered by roofs. They were traced from images taken about 2012 to 2020, so newer buildings are missing. An earlier version said 5,411,008, which counted some buildings twice and a few thousand outside New England. The inset is downtown Boston at full detail. The theme comes from Borges’s one-paragraph story about a map drawn at the same size as the empire.

Data: US Building Footprints v2 © Microsoft (ODbL)
Made with: Python (geopandas, shapely)

Day 15 · Inside out

Wen Dnalgne

A straight-faced atlas plate of New England, turned upside down and backwards, with every label set upright again. Hidden in it are about 20 quiet errors that still look legitimate. Vermont and New Hampshire have swapped places. Rhode Island is inside out, so its land is open water and Narragansett Bay is the island. Nantucket and Martha’s Vineyard have traded names. The region itself is Wen Dnalgne, each word of “New England” spelled backwards. The answer key is in the project README.

Data: US Census TIGER/Line 2025, USGS NHDPlus, SRTM15+ (Scripps Institution of Oceanography), Natural Earth, NASA Blue Marble, USGS GNIS, OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL)
Made with: Python (geopandas, matplotlib, scipy), three.js

Day 17 · Light & dark

Sunset in New England on November 17

On November 17 the sun set at 3:55 pm in Madawaska, Maine and at 4:34 pm in Stamford, Connecticut. That is just over 38 minutes apart in the same time zone. The color follows computed sunset time on a 2 km grid, with a line every five minutes, and 51 towns are labeled. Times come from the NOAA solar equations and are checked against the US Naval Observatory. They assume a flat horizon at sea level, so a hill to the west would make a real sunset earlier.

Data: NOAA solar equations, US Naval Observatory, US Census Bureau, Natural Earth, Mapzen/AWS Terrain Tiles
Made with: Python (numpy, geopandas, matplotlib), Illustrator

Day 18 · NULL

Four Towns Under the Quabbin Reservoir

In 1938 Massachusetts dissolved Dana, Enfield, Greenwich and Prescott, then flooded their valley for Boston’s drinking water. Inside the shoreline is the valley as USGS surveyors mapped it in the 1880s. Outside are the same hills today, scanned by LiDAR. Two close-ups, each about 3,000 feet across, show cellar holes, walls and roads still in the ground on dry land.

Data: USGS 15-minute survey sheets (1893–94), MassGIS LiDAR, OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL)
Made with: R (sf, terra), Python (Pillow, OpenCV, geopandas)

Day 19 · Senses: Smell

New England’s Cows

Where New England smells like cows. Each cow stands for 1,000 cattle and calves, 399,290 in all, placed on pasture or hay land in its own county. Fences mark the two counties with more cattle than people: Addison, Vermont (62,820 cattle, 37,260 people) and Orleans (29,246 and 27,546). Boston’s county has 42. The 3D inset looks at northwest Vermont from the south, and the relief is exaggerated four times.

Data: USDA NASS 2022 Census of Agriculture, US Census Bureau, USGS NLCD 2021 and Terrain Tiles, NOAA Coastal Relief Model
Made with: Python, Blender

Day 20 · Hexagons

Snowfall in an Average Year

5,236 hexagons of about 15 square miles each, every one as tall as the snow that falls on it in an average year, from the 1991–2020 NOAA normals at 313 weather stations. Hatched tops are estimates with no station within 25 miles. Mt. Washington’s summit gets 282 inches, nearly six times Boston’s 49. Edgartown on Martha’s Vineyard gets 25.

Data: NOAA NCEI U.S. Climate Normals 1991–2020, NOAA RCC-ACIS, US Census Bureau, Natural Earth, Mapzen/AWS Terrain Tiles, Uber H3
Made with: Python (h3)

Day 21 · OpenStreetMap

Every Street Suffix in New England

All 345,768 named streets in New England’s OpenStreetMap, sorted by the last word of the name. Road is first with 102,515, then Street, Lane, Drive, Avenue and Way, which together make up 86% of them. The rest is the fun part: 49 streets end in Rotary, 48 of them in Massachusetts, and at least 371 Maine streets are still named Fire Road plus a number. Counts are checked against the US Census Bureau’s 2024 road list.

Data: OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL), extract of November 17, 2026; US Census Bureau TIGER/Line 2024; NH Department of Transportation
Made with: Python (pyosmium, geopandas, matplotlib)

Day 22 · Projections

Row in a Straight Line to Australia

Push off from Portland Head Light in Cape Elizabeth, Maine, point the bow at 117° and row in a perfectly straight line. The next land is Australia, 13,886 miles away. The globe is rotated so the whole great circle runs flat across eight panels, with the seafloor under the boat. The closest calls are about 3½ miles off the Cape of Good Hope and 2 miles off Kerguelen. Click the map for the full eight-panel strip.

Data: NOAA ETOPO1, checked against GEBCO 2020; Natural Earth, geoBoundaries, US Census Bureau
Made with: Python (numba, pyproj, cartopy, matplotlib)

Day 24 · Network

Drive Time from Boston

How long it takes to drive from the Massachusetts State House to every part of New England, with no traffic. One color for every 15 minutes, and 66 cities and towns listed in order. Provincetown (2 h 23) is a longer drive than Portland, Maine (1 h 49). Madawaska, Maine is 7 h 23. The listed times were compared with a second routing engine, which puts 26 of 67 in a different 15-minute band, so read them as estimates.

Data: OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL), US Census Bureau, Natural Earth
Made with: Python (geopandas, matplotlib), OSRM, Valhalla

Day 25 · Is this a map?

The Presidential Range

A side view of the mountains around Mount Washington, New Hampshire, drawn to look like an ink painting. The land is cut into 44 slices, each about a third of a mile wide, and each line is the top of one slice. Light lines are far away and dark lines are close. The red line runs through Mount Washington, 6,288 feet. Heights are drawn four times taller than real, and the red sun is only decoration.

Data: USGS 3D Elevation Program; OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL)
Made with: Python (rasterio, NumPy, SciPy, Pillow), Illustrator

Day 26 · Water

New England, Drawn Only in Its Water

Rivers, streams, lakes, ponds, bogs and marshes, with the coast, borders and place names left off. The shape still reads, and each of the six states gets its own sheet below. A heavier line carries more water; the Connecticut River averages 17,400 cubic feet a second at Thompsonville, Connecticut. Streams that drain less than about a square mile and a half are left out.

Data: NHDPlus V2.1 (U.S. EPA and USGS), USGS National Water Information System
Made with: Python

Day 27 · New tool

Massachusetts Buildings by the Year They Were Built

Every building in 15 Massachusetts places, 238,307 in all, colored by the year it was built. The darker the color, the older the building. Boston is above and the other 14 are below. My new tool was Lonboard, a Python library that draws millions of shapes at once; I explored the data with it, then drew the final sheets in Python. Town records often say 1900 when the real year is unknown, so some older buildings hide in the 1900–1939 color.

Data: MassGIS property tax parcels and assessor records (2026); building outlines © Microsoft (ODbL); streets © OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL); US Census Bureau TIGER/Line
Made with: Python, Lonboard
